道

道michi. The way. A road you walk, not a thing you own.

The road into Kumano begins at Tanabe.

Morihei Ueshiba was born there in 1883, at the foot of the mountains the pilgrims had walked for a thousand years. He trained the sword, the spear and jujutsu, and under Sōkaku Takeda the art that would become aikido. In the last years of his life, in a village called Iwama, he taught that the purpose of budo is harmony and not victory, and that the work of a lifetime is to make that true in the body.

The art is his. The line is how it reached a hall in West London.

Daimon-zaka, the cedar steps of the Kumano Kodō.

系

系kei. The line. Four teachers, one art.

The same hands, correcting the same mistakes, for a hundred years.

Morihei Ueshiba Tanabe, 1883. The founder.

Kazuo Chiba Uchideshi to the founder at Hombu Dojo. Sent to Britain in 1966.

Minoru Kanetsuka Technical Director of the British Aikido Federation from 1978 until his death in 2019.

Arabella Braun 5th Dan, Aikikai. His student from 1988 to 2019. Teaches here.

A line is not a list of names. It is one teacher standing where the last one stood, so that what reaches the student is the art, and not an opinion of it.

Minoru Kanetsuka Sensei, 8th Dan Aikikai Shihan.

剣

剣ken. The sword. Aikido carries it in the body.

One cut, and the room is still.

The posture is the swordsman's posture. The line of entry is the line of the cut, and the hands move as if a blade were in them. On the mat the sword is white oak. It is raised, it falls once, and the hall is quiet again. The rest of the art is learning to stand where that cut cannot reach you, and then one step closer.

A bokken, drawn from its own curve. White oak, one metre and two centimetres.

墨

結musubi. To tie. The attack and the answer become one movement.

One movement, finished.

The attack is received, joined and turned, and the movement ends where it joined. Nothing is blocked and nothing is forced. On a good day it looks as if nothing happened, and the person who attacked is on the floor, unhurt.

畳

畳tatami. The mat. A hall off Brook Green.

A plain hall. The old work, done properly.

Saturday, half past nine. Tuesday evening. Small classes, so that every student is seen and corrected. The hall is plain, the bow is made, and the work is the work the line has always done: falling, rising, entering, turning, and keeping your composure while someone is trying to take it from you.

心

心kokoro. Heart, mind and spirit, which Japanese does not separate.

What is trained.

体

The body

To fall and to rise, a thousand times, until the body stops arguing and starts listening.

息

The breath

To keep it when someone is pushing, and to give it back when they stop.

眼

The attention

To see the whole room and the one person in front of you, at the same time.

心

The spirit

To meet what comes without fear or anger, and to leave the mat quieter than you arrived.

None of this is mystical. It is repetition, correction and time, in the company of people doing the same.

門

門mon. The door. Anyone may come once.

Come, bow, train, decide.

No experience, no uniform, no promise. A first class is booked online. After that there are three ways in: come once more, learn for six weeks, or be invited to join.
